\section{Theoretical Analysis}\label{sec:theory}

This section answers three design questions: when SOMA may accept the session-local surrogate, when its warm-start overhead is amortized, and how many soft-prompt candidates are needed. \rev{SOMA is effective because later turns remain tied to this warm-start state, not because later turns are intrinsically easier than early ones. The results characterize operating conditions; they do not claim an advantage over alternative mining strategies, which we assess empirically in Section~\ref{sec:exp}. Full statements and proofs are given in Appendix~\ref{sec:app_proof}.}

\subsection{When to Switch}\label{sec:warm-start}

At turn $t$, let $S_t=\mathcal D_{t-1}\oplus q_t$ be the shared context, and let $a_t^F$ and $a_t^G$ be the responses of the original model and of the adapted surrogate. We measure their discrepancy with the bounded score $\mathsf{Gap}_{\Theta}(S_t)=1-\cos\big(\phi(a_t^G),\phi(a_t^F)\big)\in[0,1]$, where $\phi$ is a fixed sentence encoder and $\Theta$ is the current adapter. Because consecutive turns are dependent, all bounds use effective sample sizes, $W_{\mathrm{eff}}$ for the warm-start window and $|B|_{\mathrm{eff}}$ for an acceptance batch, which shrink when the early turns are noisy or topic-mixed. Lemma~\ref{lem:warmstart} shows that any bounded warm-start statistic concentrates around its mean at rate $\sqrt{2\log(2/\delta)/W_{\mathrm{eff}}}$, which is why SOMA demands a longer warm start when the early turns do not define a coherent local state.

The switching decision compares the surrogate before and after adaptation. On an acceptance batch $B$, let $X_j=\mathsf{Gap}_{\Theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(S_j)-\mathsf{Gap}_{\Theta_{\mathrm{new}}}(S_j)$ and $\widehat\Delta_B=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{S_j\in B}X_j$, so that a positive $\widehat\Delta_B$ means that adaptation reduced the gap on the batch.

\begin{theorem}[Acceptance Bound for Switching]\label{thm:detection}
Assume $X_j\in[-1,1]$ and let $\gamma_B(\delta)=\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)/|B|_{\mathrm{eff}}}$. With probability at least $1-\delta$, $\mathbb E[X]\ge \widehat\Delta_B-\gamma_B(\delta)$. Hence, if $\widehat\Delta_B\ge \varepsilon+\gamma_B(\delta)$, the true expected improvement is at least $\varepsilon$ with confidence $1-\delta$.
\end{theorem}

Combining both bounds yields SOMA's switching rule (Corollary~\ref{cor:switch}): choose $W$ so that the warm-start uncertainty is at most $\eta$ and $|B|$ so that $\gamma_B(\delta)\le\gamma$, and switch only when both the output-fidelity gate and the locality gate pass with margins larger than $\eta+\gamma$, which controls the decision error at confidence $1-2\delta$. An abrupt topic change shifts the locality statistic, fails the gate, and triggers an immediate rollback to the original model.

\subsection{When Does SOMA Give Net Efficiency Gains?}\label{sec:theory-cost}

SOMA pays a one-time warm-start overhead, so its efficiency must be judged per session. Let $T$ be the number of turns, $W$ the switch point, and $c_F(\cdot)$ and $c_G(\cdot)$ the per-turn costs (latency, tokens, or price) of the original model and of the surrogate on its compressed input $\widetilde S_t$. Relative to serving every turn with $F$, the net gain of SOMA is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:net-gain}
\Delta C(T;W)=\sum_{t=W+1}^{T}\Big[c_F(\mathcal D_{t-1}\oplus q_t)-c_G(\widetilde S_t)\Big]
-C_{\mathrm{probe}}(W)-C_{\mathrm{LoRA}}(W)-C_{\mathrm{rb}}(T;W),
\end{equation}
where $C_{\mathrm{probe}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{LoRA}}$ are the costs of soft-prompt mining and localized adaptation, and $C_{\mathrm{rb}}$ covers drift checks and occasional rollback. SOMA is therefore beneficial only when the accumulated post-switch savings exceed the one-time overhead. \rev{This explains the operating range measured in Table~\ref{tab:break-even}: sessions of 1--4 turns do not benefit, whereas locally coherent sessions of 5 or more turns amortize the initialization cost, with the gain growing in session length.}

\subsection{How Many Soft-Prompt Candidates?}

Near a reference prompt, assume that the mining objective is locally quadratic with curvature $\mathbf H_T\succeq 0$ whose energy concentrates in an $r_{\mathrm{act}}$-dimensional active subspace with dominant unit direction $\mathbf u_1$. SOMA draws $M$ random candidates and keeps the one with the largest mining loss. If $p_{r_{\mathrm{act}}}(\theta)$ denotes the probability that one uniform random direction lies within angle $\theta$ of $\mathbf u_1$, then the best of $M$ candidates does so with probability $1-(1-p_{r_{\mathrm{act}}}(\theta))^M$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:coverage}), so coverage $1-\delta$ is guaranteed once $M\ge\lceil \log\delta/\log(1-p_{r_{\mathrm{act}}}(\theta))\rceil$ (Corollary~\ref{col:M}); for $r_{\mathrm{act}}=1$ one candidate suffices. A candidate within angle $\theta$ of $\mathbf u_1$ retains at least a $\cos^2\theta$ fraction of the top curvature (Lemma~\ref{lem:subopt}), which turns the candidate count into an explicit quality trade-off. We set $W$, $|B|$, and $M$ from these bounds in all experiments (Appendix~\ref{sec:app_implementation}).
