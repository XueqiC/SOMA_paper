\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Notations}

A multi-turn dialogue prefix of length $k$ is $\mathcal{D}_k=\{(q_1,a_1),\ldots,(q_k,a_k)\}$, where $q_t$ is the user query at turn $t$ and $a_t$ is the model response. We write $F$ for the original model, a black-box LLM as in MLaaS, and $G$ for the small surrogate; the response of $M\in\{F,G\}$ at turn $t$ is $a_t^M$. \rev{We write $S_t=\mathcal D_{t-1}\oplus q_t$ for the dialogue state at turn $t$, i.e., the history followed by the new query.} A soft prompt of length $L$ is a matrix $\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}$. Appendix~\ref{sec:notation} lists all symbols used in the paper.

\subsection{Exploring Token--Turn Patterns in Multi-Turn LLM Dialogues}
\label{sec:long_tail}

\begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{0.42\textwidth}
  \vspace{-11pt}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.419\textwidth]{figure/long_tail.pdf}
  \vspace{-18pt}
  \caption{Average token count per turn, normalized by Turn~1, on four dialogue datasets: usage drops after the early turns and forms a long tail.}
  \vspace{-11pt}
  \label{fig:longtail-token-decay}
\end{wrapfigure}
Standard serving processes each new request together with the entire history, so the cost of a turn grows with the length of the conversation. The amount of \emph{new} information in a turn, however, need not grow at all. We therefore first study token usage across turns.


We examine four representative settings, which together cover different speaker roles, task goals, and interaction structures: \textit{ShareGPT}~\citep{sharegpt2023} for open-ended human--LLM chat, \textit{ReMeDi}~\citep{yan2022remedi} for doctor--patient consultation, \textit{Craigslist Bargain}~\citep{he2018decoupling} for negotiation, and \textit{Multi-Character}~\citep{agentlans_multi-character_dialogue_2024} for multi-party role-play. For each dataset, we compute the average token count at turn \(t\) and normalize it by the average of Turn~1 in that dataset.

Figure~\ref{fig:longtail-token-decay} shows a consistent long-tail pattern. Early turns are token-heavy because they introduce the task, the topic, the constraints, and the speaker's intent; later turns are much shorter and settle at a low, stable level. ShareGPT and ReMeDi peak at the opening turn, whereas Craigslist and Multi-Character peak shortly after it, once the negotiation or role-play is set up. Shorter does not mean independent: many later turns are local updates to an established dialogue state, so the marginal new input shrinks while standard serving keeps re-processing the full prefix. This mismatch motivates an adaptive strategy: let the original model establish the early dialogue state, and hand subsequent turns to a cheaper surrogate for as long as they stay within that state.

\subsection{Local Manifold Approximation for Multi-Turn Dialogues}

The long-tail pattern creates an opportunity for surrogate serving, but a naive substitution is unreliable. A later turn is short because the preceding dialogue supplies much of its meaning, so a surrogate that is misaligned with the original model under that prefix can give fluent but drifting answers.

\rev{We formalize this challenge with a local view of the session. Given a prefix \(\mathcal{D}_{k}\), let \(\mathcal Q_k\) be the distribution of the dialogue states \(S_t=\mathcal D_{t-1}\oplus q_t\), \(t>k\), that the session visits afterwards. We call its support the local region, or local manifold, induced by the prefix. Since the hidden spaces of \(F\) and \(G\) differ in dimension and have no canonical correspondence, we compare the two models through their responses. A fixed sentence encoder \(\phi\) embeds both, and \(\mathsf{Gap}_G(S)=1-\cos\big(\phi(a^G(S)),\phi(a^F(S))\big)\in[0,1]\).} The goal is a local rather than a global match, which is what makes a much smaller surrogate viable.

\begin{problem}[Local Manifold Approximation for Multi-Turn Interactions]\label{prob:lma}
\rev{Given a prefix \(\mathcal{D}_{k}\) and the original model's responses \(a_1^F,\ldots,a_k^F\), learn a surrogate \(G\in\mathcal{G}\) that minimizes the expected discrepancy on the turns that follow:
\[
\min_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\; R_k(G)=\mathbb E_{S\sim\mathcal Q_k}\big[\mathsf{Gap}_G(S)\big].
\]}
\end{problem}

\rev{Only the first \(k\) turns are observed, so the problem is solvable only if they carry enough supervision for the later ones. The local view makes this testable. While later states stay close to the warm-start states, the gap on warm-start turns should track the gap on later turns, so post-switch similarity should rise with the warm-start length, level off, and fall once the topic shifts (Figures~\ref{fig:operating}(c) and~\ref{fig:app_switching}). In our data, goal-anchored dialogues such as medical consultations and negotiations seem to meet this premise better than reasoning-heavy or multi-party ones (Section~\ref{sec:exp}).}
